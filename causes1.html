Climate Change

Why Is the Climate Changing So Fast?

Climate change is accelerating at an unprecedented pace due to human activities. Fossil fuel combustion, deforestation, and industrial-scale agriculture release massive amounts of greenhouse gases that trap heat and destabilize Earth’s climate systems.

  • CO₂ accumulates for centuries
  • Methane traps heat far more efficiently
  • Forests lose their carbon-absorbing power
  • Extreme weather becomes the new normal

Causes of Climate Change

Climate change occurs due to a combination of natural factors and human activities. However, scientific evidence clearly shows that human activities are the primary driver of the rapid climate change observed today.

Human Activities

The burning of coal, oil, and natural gas releases large amounts of CO₂ and other greenhouse gases, which trap heat in the atmosphere and drive global warming.

Deforestation reduces the Earth’s ability to absorb carbon dioxide and disrupts natural ecosystems that regulate the climate.

Industrial production of goods, energy, and food generates high emissions and releases waste that pollutes air, water, and soil.

Vehicles powered by fossil fuels emit CO₂ and significantly contribute to air pollution, especially in densely populated urban areas.

Food production, particularly cattle farming, releases methane (CH₄), a greenhouse gas far more effective than CO₂ at trapping heat.

Plastic production and waste management increase greenhouse gas emissions and cause long-term damage to terrestrial and marine ecosystems.

Natural Factors

Volcanic eruptions release gases and particles into the atmosphere that can temporarily influence global climate patterns.

Changes in the intensity of solar radiation can affect Earth’s temperature over long natural cycles.

Natural variations in Earth’s orbit and rotation influence how solar energy is distributed across the planet.

Climate Change in Reality

Climate change is no longer a distant threat. Across Indonesia and the world, its impacts are already unfolding through extreme weather, environmental damage, and humanitarian crises.

Indonesia

Jakarta Flooding

Jakarta is one of the cities most vulnerable to climate change. Increasingly erratic and extreme rainfall has led to recurrent major flooding events. According to reports, the 2020 floods inundated more than 300 locations across Jakarta, caused dozens of fatalities, and forced thousands of residents to evacuate. In addition to urban planning issues, the rising intensity of rainfall linked to climate change has significantly worsened these conditions.

Indonesia

Forest Fires in Kalimantan and Sumatra

Longer and drier dry seasons driven by climate change have intensified forest and peatland fires. In 2019, widespread fires in Kalimantan and Sumatra produced thick haze that spread across Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore. The impacts included severe ecosystem damage and major public health problems, with millions affected by respiratory illnesses such as acute respiratory infections (ARI).

Indonesia

Extreme Heat Waves

Approximately 48.6 million people in Indonesia around 17% of the total population were exposed to extreme heat between December 2024 and February 2025, a period that typically coincides with the rainy season. According to Climate Central, this phenomenon represents a direct consequence of fossil fuel combustion, which has intensified global warming.

Indonesia

Floods and Landslides in Early 2025

Data from Indonesia’s National Disaster Management Agency (BNPB) indicate an increasing trend in hydrometeorological disasters nationwide. In early 2025, many regions experienced severe flooding and landslides. Changes in weather patterns associated with climate extremes are a major factor contributing to the rising frequency and intensity of these disasters.

Global

Arctic Ice Melt

Data from NASA and the National Snow and Ice Data Center show that Arctic sea ice reached its tenth lowest extent on record. Both the Arctic and Antarctic continue to lose ice mass, contributing to global sea level rise. This trend threatens coastal cities worldwide, including Jakarta, Bangkok, and New York.

Global

Drought in East Africa

Countries such as Kenya, Ethiopia, and Somalia have experienced longer and more intense dry seasons. Climate change has worsened food insecurity in the region, leaving millions dependent on humanitarian aid. Extreme drought conditions have also triggered conflicts over scarce water and land resources.

Global

Super Typhoon Haiyan

In 2013, the Philippines was struck by Super Typhoon Haiyan, one of the most powerful tropical storms ever recorded. Warmer ocean temperatures linked to climate change amplified the storm’s intensity. Haiyan killed more than 6,000 people, destroyed critical infrastructure, and left millions homeless.

Global

Record Global Temperatures

According to Carbon Brief, 2024 became the first year in which the global average temperature reached 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels, exceeding the Paris Agreement target. Projections suggest that 2025 will be among the hottest years ever recorded.
